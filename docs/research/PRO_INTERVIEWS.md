# How the great duel players say duel is played

Research notes, 2026-11-13. What the best Quake duel players have said, in interviews, podcasts, their own
match breakdowns and lessons, about how to win a duel. Written for BobbyBones: the aim is to understand the
game the way its best players understand it, so the bot's design (what he can know, what he decides, what he
is paid for) matches the real game.

A companion file, `DUEL_STRATEGY.md`, covers general strategy guides (item control, map control). This file
stays with the players' own words and reasoning.

**How to read this.** Almost everything here is summarised in my own words from sources I actually read
(transcripts of YouTube videos taken from the auto-captions, and web articles). Short quotes are marked and
attributed. Auto-captions garble names and words; I have only kept points whose meaning was clear. Every
source is listed in the last section with what I could and could not read. Where a player's view comes
second-hand (an article or a forum post describing what he said), it is marked as such.

**Names.** Two corrections to the brief: ZeRo4 is John Hill (USA), confirmed by his MCV and Shacknews
interviews; Johan Quick is **toxjq** (Sweden), a different player. k1llsen is Marcel Paul (Germany) in
the sources I read (ESReality's "Grilled" interview), not Nikita Marchinsky. czm's full name was not given in
any source I read (he was a Princeton/Caltech student by his own account), so I have not added one.

## 1. The players

### 1.1 rapha (Shane Hendrixson, USA)

**Who.** Started competing in 2008, sixteen major duel titles (Wikipedia), many QuakeCon wins, IEM IV
world champion, then Quake Champions champion (QPL era). Played for SK Gaming, then Team Liquid. Widely
called the greatest duel player. Nicknamed "five steps ahead" after an SK-Gaming interview. Through most
of Quake Live he played a balanced, control-heavy, defensive style; in Quake Champions' time-limit duel he
switched to a much more aggressive tempo. He modelled his early game on ZeRo4 and Cooller.

Sources: his 2.7-hour interview with dkt (Daniel Kapadia), his ESL Classics breakdown of his IEM IV final
map against Cooller, Thooorin's "Reflections", SteelSeries, Team Liquid, Wikipedia (all in section 6).

**Theory of duel in one line.** Asked for duel theory in a few minutes, he said the answer is simple:
die less than the other guy within the time limit. How you do that is the combination of mechanics and
fundamentals with using the map's items to your advantage, knowing when you are in control and when you are
out of it. It is "a constant ebb and flow of decision making" about who has the advantage from position and
weapons, and whether a stack is big enough to beat the usual advantage of a position. He later framed his
decisions in poker terms: positive and negative expected value, constantly updated.

**Item control and timing.**
- He names his timing as his biggest edge over other top players: he times more items, more exactly, for
  longer, and so makes fewer mistakes of leaving items up.
- In Quake Live he tracked "three to four items at a time", never below two, while also tracking where
  the opponent was. His view: players who time well but do not read the opponent "play the map" and are
  predictable; players who read the opponent but do not time get lost on the map (his example: cypher for
  a long time timed only the mega exactly and would sometimes miss a red armor and leave the area). He
  credits doing both at once as his main edge for years.
- In the IEM IV final (ESL Classics) he narrates timing constantly: he saw Cooller take the red "between 32
  and 33" so knew it returned at 58; heard the red pickup sound to get its timing while out of control;
  kept timing the mega "even though I'm out of control"; and played to make Cooller take an item as close
  to its spawn second as possible, so the next timings were predictable.
- Timing out of control has a specific purpose: keep timing the items the opponent owns so you can "find when
  they're synced up" and take one when he cannot cover both.
- Even timing decays without play: after a two-week break his aim feels fine but it is "harder for me to
  mentally time as many items", so he spends two or three days practising nothing but timing.
- In Quake Champions both players sometimes stop timing the light armors because neither wants them, so the
  constant four-to-five item timing of Quake Live does not apply.

**Control as a cycle, not as hoarding (QC).** In QC, armor has soft caps, so you cannot "just keep collecting
items". He found that controlling all the armors applied more pressure than controlling the mega (example:
Corrupted Keep's two light armors plus heavy armor). Repeated small fights let you soak light armors before
grabbing the heavy, ending a cycle with more armor than the opponent. He tied this to the damage split: armor
absorbs two thirds, health one third; in an all-in lightning fight with equal damage dealt, the mega-health
player (175 health, 100 armor) wins over the armor player (100 health, 175 armor). So the armor-stacked
player wants many small fights and recovers health from small items; the mega player wants all-ins. This
works only if your timing is excellent; otherwise you overcommit for light armors and die. A correct
opponent counters by hiding for two full cycles and dealing damage after you have spent health on rockets.

**Being ahead, being behind, and the value of a life.**
- His mentor ZeRo4 taught him: "don't play to win, you play to not lose." Play as if you have already won and
  make whatever choice best avoids losing in that moment, which is sometimes sound control and sometimes going
  all in.
- He "maximized my value when I died": before a fight he asks what happens if it goes wrong. If I die, can I
  get control back right away on respawn? If I survive low, which weapons and routes are still mine? To kill
  him an opponent should have to pay the highest price.
- Expecting to be in control all game is "arrogant and greedy"; against the best, full control happens maybe
  one game in twenty. Prepare to make the most of control while you have it and to work back when down.
- The classic error of the leader: feeling a two-frag lead is not enough, forcing a third frag in a weak spot,
  and then being locked out of the map, so one misstep lets the other player run the table for five frags.
- On tilt: after the opponent pulls off something great, or after you botch an easy fight, the urge is to
  gamble to "make back" the lost chance; that gamble fails and the bad spawn that follows makes it worse.
- At the end of a won game (IEM IV): he attacks only when it is a fight he is "100%" sure to win twice over, and
  otherwise runs the clock; with 20 seconds left he knew the opponent would take 200 damage to reach him.

**Leaving the opponent alive.** Because he times better, he notices in fights that "I should actually leave
him alive now" where other players instinctively finish the kill; they are not watching the bigger picture
(where he would respawn, which item is about to come up).

**Reading the opponent.**
- Basketball taught him "to see the play unfold before it even happens" (SteelSeries). His main strength,
  in his words, is getting into the opponent's head and anticipating several steps ahead.
- Often "I see exactly where they are in my head all the time" (Reflections), which got him wall-hack
  accusations.
- In the IEM IV final he: heard one footstep at the bottom of the stairs and fired a rocket where Cooller would
  be; listened to confirm Cooller was not jumping, which meant he had stayed put and the red was free;
  delayed Cooller at the mega by two seconds to grab an armor; chose the rail over the red so Cooller would
  not get a free rail and be able to play "extra defensive" and run.
- His weakness when young, per ZeRo4: playing "too cute", making hard reads and hard counters that leave no
  leeway. Even if right, a slight misread leaves you out of the map; too many all-ins.
- Against a style he cannot practise (Evil, in Europe), he studied demos and found the weakness (Evil struggled
  with rail-heavy players), so he added rail to his own game against him. He tried to build an internal "alarm"
  for when staying in a fight against Evil is a bait into death.
- He plays the first five minutes of a series "touch and go" to feel out the opponent, then adapts. He adapts
  tempo to the opponent: faster against a player who does not time well (catch him on rotation), slower
  against an impatient player.

**Positioning.** From ZeRo4 he learned "the utmost importance that positioning has in quake". On dm6 he keeps
the high ground over the mega and red so he can damage either and run if needed; his narration repeatedly
values "I didn't take any damage because I knew where to be".

**Weapons.** On dm6, rail matters even against a non-rail player; Cooller denied him the rocket launcher, "the
weapon that I need to play defensive". When out of control, getting the rail let him fight from range and chip.
Against Evil, more rail. In practice he bans himself from weapons or items (only five rails all game on a close
map; no red armor all game on ZTN, use the red as bait; or give up the mega and learn to punish whoever takes it)
to learn how to get control back without a key resource.

**Aim.** By his own account, for most of his career "pretty much everybody else aimed better than me in the top
eight", so he had to outsmart people. His aim improved in QC (new mice, team modes). Rapha says he reads the game
rather than relying on reflexes (Wikipedia paraphrase). On setup: grip and mouse shape matter for tension and
injury; ergonomics over trends.

**Practice and review.** Quality over quantity; every practice played "as if it is match time", with
self-imposed penalties for losing focus. Play out lost practice games to the end to learn comebacks (he did
this more than others). Never practise against one player too much: it builds habits that only beat that
player (learned at ESWC Masters of Paris after a week's bootcamp with ZeRo4). Mix opponents, including weaker
ones. Above all, review: once mechanics are high, gains come from "how you're thinking about the game", from
watching yourself as your own brutally honest coach, and from studying others' demos.

**Mental.** Duel is mentally the hardest game; one lapse loses a map and there is no teammate or timeout.
Keep problem-solving in the moment; do not give in to frustration (lag, an opponent's crazy shots). A game
lost by one or two frags under bad conditions means you played well.

### 1.2 cypher (Alexei Yanushevsky, Belarus)

**Who.** Quake Live era "big four" (with rapha, Cooller, Av3k), QuakeCon champion (2012, 2014 per commentary
titles), still winning Quake Live leagues in 2025 (beat AGENT). Known for a sneaky, defensive, accurate,
rail-and-trap style that punishes overextension (Evil describes him as "always sneaky", "not too aggressive").
He says Quake Live is the game he played most; it is "like riding a bike".

Sources: Endpoint Quake videos with Av3k (two breakdowns), Plus Forward and NAVI interviews, a 2017
cybersport.com interview relayed on ESReality.

**Quake reduces to stack, weapons and timings.** He says Quake comes down to "stack, weapons and timings"
(Plus Forward). Players who give him room get caught out in those areas.

**Optimal places and habits.** After many pro games each player has "optimal places" when in control:
shortcuts to every item, predicting the opponent. Everyone's way of controlling is unique, with habits of
where they go, how, and at what timing they push. To get back in, you need "perfect timings" while out of
control, and knowledge of the opponent's habits, so you know the "holes" where he overextends. You hear
the sound of him overextending and know you will be faster to a given area for the next five seconds.

**Out of control: play not to lose.** His most quoted line (repeated by Av3k): when defending, "I'm not trying
to win, I'm trying not to lose". You cannot play defence while thinking how you will win; trying not to lose
means dealing as much damage as possible safely, and sometimes the kill comes by itself.
- Do not fight at a spawning item when you know it is spawning; be elsewhere. You cannot control a whole map;
  the opponent who is timing well becomes predictable, and an opponent who gets impatient starts leaving items
  to chase you, which opens holes.
- The defender who holds an angle and backs off as he shoots ("playing backwards") always hits more than the
  attacker who walks into him; this "defensive damage" stops the attacker killing you, and you can leave the
  spot whenever you want.
- With 17 HP on Battleforged against AGENT he skipped every item he could be trapped at, stayed in "corridors
  where I can escape always", kept the mega timing, and won the fight that came.
- "Timing for me is much more important when I don't have control"; Av3k agreed that the biggest timing skill
  is timing with no control. With control you run the cycle; without it, timings tell you where the
  opponent must be, so you can use spare time to change position.

**The fight decided before it starts: stack arithmetic.** Discussing Serious (a top aimer), cypher and Av3k
argue that heavy aimers think "how to hit" instead of where and when. With equal-ish hitting at the top level,
the stacked player wins: count your stack, count the enemy's, and you know who wins the fight before it
starts. Av3k: rapha knows his natural LG is around 35%; if he only fights when he has 30-50% more stack, a 45%
LG opponent still loses. Serious sometimes takes fights against 200/200 opponents trusting his aim and dies to
the stack. Cypher's diagnosis of Serious: his timings are "reversed": he holds back when he should push and
pushes when he should wait.

**Prediction and tempo.** Everything in Quake is done ahead: "you can't really live in the moment"; if you
do, you are lost for the next moves. Use sound, understand the opponent's speed and how he runs the map, and
read items: an opponent who has not eaten megas or armors is an easy target.

**A top-down picture.** Look at the game from above "like a 3D map", imagine yourself as a point on it.
Av3k says this idea came first from Cooller, who told him he sees the game as a 3D map.

**Practice.** Against medium players you practise your strengths (aim, spawn fragging, not losing frags);
against top players you practise your weaknesses (mental, hard situations). Too much practice against much
stronger players demotivates and breeds bad habits; he avoids practising with the strongest just before
events (2017). He sets goals like playing a medium opponent as if he would punish every mistake, so taking
fights becomes natural; but beware habits that work only against weak players (they die to the good ones).
Improvement comes from being punished, mostly at LAN.

**Other views.** "Sometimes the first frag does not solve anything" (NAVI 2019): the respawned player can come
back with the advantage. Against AGENT, the hardest thing is his speed of decision; giving him full control
means losing quickly; AGENT runs items efficiently so he is somewhat predictable. Against Pavel, you can give up
an item and still be caught elsewhere. He wants map pools to include one map without rail and one without LG.
He fakes a rocket jump (sound) to bait a reaction. Plasma or LG fired near the head "blinds" the opponent
(an old toxjq idea he repeats with Av3k); Cooller spammed plasma heavily on ZTN.

### 1.3 Cooller (Anton Singov, Russia)

**Who.** Quake 3 legend (ESWC champion), Quake Live big four, QC champion (PGL era and later). rapha
describes him as a constrictor: "a boa or a python". Cooller's focus is to run the items and control the map,
damage you when he can, and make sure you are suffocating the whole game. ZTN was the map that showed it best.

Sources: ESL Classics episode where he analyses his IEM IV final map (dm13) against rapha; rapha's ESL
Classics; descriptions by rapha, Av3k, cypher, k1llsen; a Russian podcast (mostly about life; little game
content).

**His own breakdown of dm13 vs rapha.**
- He sees rapha as "very tactical" and "very positional": rapha takes good positions and waits for the
  opponent's mistake. To beat him you must control positions, time every item you control, and catch him in
  the map's entrances (with LG).
- Asked what is the worst mistake against rapha: "every mistake can cause the game".
- From the spawn: listen to understand where the opponent is and what he will do. He heard rapha take a yellow
  and inferred he was going for the rocket launcher and then the mega; then heard him going for the second
  yellow, so he had time for the mega himself.
- Once in control he calls out every mega timing ("next mega at 28") and prepares for the mega fight in
  advance. He takes small health too: "every health is important".
- Without the rocket launcher you "can't move around the map quickly", so you must be slower.
- Counting the opponent's rockets: not really, but sometimes in a close fight.
- He knows the stack split: "I have 200 health and 100 armor but rapha has 100 health and 200 armor".
- He loses control by losing one fight ("one mistake, two frags"; five minutes of work wasted). Then: be slower,
  get the two yellows.
- Taking the mega knowing he would die, so as not to let rapha have full control: he accepts one frag to
  deny the stack.
- When the control player keeps having to come back for items, the out-of-control player "knows when you're
  going to be in which areas" and goes to the others (the commentator's summary of rapha against him).
- He says winning needs some luck too.

**Seen by others.** Av3k: Cooller sees the game as a 3D map from above. rapha: Cooller is better at item
cycles than exact timing (ESR forum, second-hand); he "hates losing". k1llsen: Cooller uses trash talk and
mind games before the match. Av3k: Cooller's LG was outstanding from the start, though his rail was weak early
in Q3. cypher: Cooller spammed plasma heavily for the mega fight on ZTN.

### 1.4 k1llsen (Marcel Paul, Germany)

**Who.** Quake Live top-eight player known as a railgun specialist, often called streaky; became a dominant
player of QC's time-limit duel and the QPL (several QPL wins, QuakeCon 2019 per video titles). rapha describes
his QC style as "the opposite" of his own aggression: super calculated, centre-based, slow tempo, "very bity,
very trap heavy", smooth tempo. Both styles won.

Sources: Thooorin's Reflections with k1llsen; descriptions by rapha, toxjq, cypher.

**His own reflections.**
- In Quake Live he was "quite an aggressive player" and that made him vulnerable; with today's knowledge he
  would have been more successful. When a match became tight, he would lose his nerve and try an aggressive
  move "that I don't have anything to back up"; the best players knew he would.
- He lacked the ability to readjust inside a match. Now, if something goes wrong, he readjusts, and readjusts
  again until it works.
- Rail stays his favourite weapon, but "I just know how accurate I am" now; he chooses fights knowing his
  real accuracy. Players learned to play around his rail as they learned to play around strenx's LG.
- On rapha: the hard part is stopping his "flow"; once rapha has control he rotates so well around the map
  that few openings remain. The only way is to find small openings, push him a little out of control, and keep
  it close.
- On Evil: his mental is so strong you cannot pull an emotion out of him; Evil keeps his flow even running low
  on HP. His one weakness: punishing mistakes aggressively.
- His best tournament: he understood "every single sound cue" and timed the opponent's movements almost
  perfectly. If no sound came from an area or around a corner, he knew what the opponent was doing next.
- Practice by streaming taught opponents his habits (Evil read him "like a book" after many weekly cups), so
  he stopped streaming most practice.
- Round-based QC duel (three lives) made every decision matter so much that players avoided risk and
  overtimes became long. In time-limit duel "there's always time to catch up if you keep things close".

**Seen by others.** toxjq: rail specialists like Stermy and k1llsen are excellent standing still and hitting
angles, and weaker when play is fast and unexpected. cypher (2021): k1llsen showed he can lose, so it is
possible to play at his level.

### 1.5 czm (USA; real name not confirmed in sources read)

**Who.** Early-2000s Quake 3 star (QuakeCon 2004 grand final against ZeRo4), came back for Quake Live (top
four QuakeCon 2011: beat Spart1e and DaHanG, lost to rapha, bronze final vs Cooller). Remembered on ESReality as
"more accurate than a clock" for timing, and in a Reddit comment for rail and for estimating enemy health
(second-hand). rapha names czm among players who timed the opponent's health before him.

Sources: two short interviews (IEM V Hannover, QuakeCon 2011). I found no czm coaching series or "czm
teaches" content; if it exists it was not reachable.

**What he said.**
- What separates players is consistent play over a long period, which builds "latent mental infrastructure"
  for thinking about the game and focusing on it.
- Mechanics must be "down to the subconscious level". His aim and rail and timing were fine, but because he was
  out of practice he had to "devote a lot of resources" to them, so he was "not quite as aware of the game".
  Awareness of the game is what consistent play buys.
- To stay focused in a match he talked to himself out loud through the game, so that he was thinking about the
  game and not something else.
- He held an 11-2 lead with four minutes to go against Spart1e and "somewhat poorly" held on (won narrowly):
  holding a lead is its own skill.
- He lost a map against rapha after a missed plasma jump to a health bubble cost him the mega and evened the
  stacks: one small execution error in a resource race can flip control.

### 1.6 ZeRo4 (John Hill, USA)

**Who.** Dominant in Quake 3 in the early 2000s (QuakeCon 2001 final vs Fatal1ty, ESWC 2003 final vs Cooller),
rapha's mentor and bootcamp partner, later worked with id Software on Quake Champions.

Sources: QuakeCon 2013 interview (full transcript); rapha's account of his teaching; MCV interview.

**What he said and taught.**
- What holds back a strong player is "purely his decision making"; everyone's aim is basically on a par at the
  top. "You don't have to go for that red armor", you don't have to take that decision; the mind game is what
  takes a player to the next level.
- Of Vo0 (2013): his CPMA-style running collects armor but "can't do much damage"; he needs more thought on
  "where the player is and what's the player's next move".
- Of Destruct: the opposite problem, he buries himself in one position and lets the opponent rotate around the
  map. Neither pure running nor pure holding works.
- Of Evil: "he knows when to push, he knows when he has the edge"; his old Russian ZTN style of staying low near
  the weapons threw ZeRo4 off.
- His teaching to rapha (via rapha): play not to lose; avoid hard reads with no leeway; have a defensive game
  for days when your normal game is not working; positioning matters above all; don't stress over what goes
  wrong.
- Duel is "like chess, or a boxing match" (MCV).

### 1.7 Raisy (Myztro, QC era)

**Who.** One of the top QC duel players (QuakeCon 2020 and 2021 finals vs vengeurR, many finals lost to rapha).
Started very aggressive (Anarki/Sorlag), then became known, and criticised, for passive play: take a frag and
hide.

Sources: Open Cyber League "Field of View" interview (keyword sections of the transcript).

**What he said.**
- He adapted to the game: when hit registration improved and abilities were nerfed, speed stopped protecting
  you, and the game became about position. He had to learn to play "slower, safer, less risky, more
  calculated": wait, camp corners, walk a lot, "hear my opponent out".
- When the opponent has control, weapons, ability and position, "you cannot really do anything but to camp and
  wait", chip damage when you can, turn the control if possible, "but if it's too risky don't risk it". Often
  waiting another heavy-armor and mega cycle is the right choice.
- He also goes passive with a frag lead because the round timer means only the frag count matters at the end.
  But overly passive play "gives away the control for free", which is also a mistake: the opponent can punish
  it.
- He will not take a risk to avoid looking boring: "if I want to win I have to play the certain ways".
- Aim is important but "not the most important aspect": players like clawz and toxjq depend on aim more;
  rapha has strong aim "but he doesn't depend on it" because positioning, item control and reading the opponent
  make up for it.
- He does not study opponents' play styles in depth because top players change their play by opponent; he notes
  their champions, positions they like, maps they prefer.
- Mental: if a fight goes wrong because you were unlucky, "let go of it immediately" and focus on the next one.
  He does not grind between matches; he prepares before the event and stays fresh.

### 1.8 toxjq (Johan Quick, Sweden)

**Who.** Quake 4 world champion era, QuakeCon 2007, QC; famed for aim (cited by Raisy as an aim-reliant
player; QC clips of 60%+ LG).

Sources: Endpoint Quake interview with Av3k, part 2 (keyword sections).

**What he said.**
- His strength was learning "quite fast how other players were doing" from demos: many players repeat the same
  thing without knowing it ("nine times out of 10 he goes to jump pad"). Then "you can set up traps and do so
  much damage without taking a risk", and out of control you can avoid that player.
- Players differ in fight habits: some do the "double back" (hit, run, come back), some fight one full fight
  until death, some always try to come back for a rail. Wait a few seconds and shoot a rocket where the
  returning player will appear and you get a free kill. If a player always runs the other way, you can take an
  item even very low because he will not return.
- Among the best players this becomes layers of bluff: you expect him to come through the teleporter and camp
  it; he knows you know; the "double fake" can confuse even yourself.
- He avoided bootcamps and practising too much with top rivals: he did not want to show how he played,
  especially his route-based play.
- "A lot of aim in Quake is also about positioning": someone may look out-aimed, but "there is no way to win
  a fight in that angle". Experience is knowing when to fight and when not to.
- Aim: most players practise the small left-right adjustment; strafing long in one direction, or going behind
  them, breaks their aim. Strenx's very low sensitivity made his LG great inside a small screen movement but weak
  for big, fast changes.
- In practice duel you must hit "to a certain degree"; otherwise the practice is useless, because you cannot do
  anything smart if you cannot defend yourself.

### 1.9 Vo0 (Netherlands; Painkiller world tour champion, QL, QC runner-up)

Sources: Thooorin's Reflections with Vo0 (keyword sections; mostly about Painkiller), Inven interview
(QuakeCon 2017), short notes.

- His Painkiller style: "always be on the move", never stand still (you are easy to hit), time the gold armor
  and mega, get favourable positions, "always having an escape route", retreat from a fight at full speed
  straight to an item.
- His rival Fatal1ty (per Vo0) copied that style, timed well and was "fairly good at spawn fragging": after a
  kill he listened for where you spawned and hunted you.
- A lost fight he should have won: at 200/200 control, an attacker with 150/150 knocked him back with a rocket and
  he hit himself with splash; the out-stacked player won. Stack advantage is a probability, not a certainty.
- QC (Inven): as Anarki against a tank, avoid face-to-face LG duels; chip with hit-and-run damage. His Anarki
  "fights backwards" while retreating, a skill he says most lack. Learn the map layout first, copy pros' routes
  on an empty map, then practise against people.
- Nerves hurt more with hitscan than with Painkiller's projectiles; when his rail stopped hitting in the final
  he lost confidence and should have kept trusting it.

### 1.10 Evil (Sergei Orekhov, Russia)

Dominant in Europe in the early 2010s; QuakeCon 2012 champion per rapha; repeatedly beaten by rapha at QuakeCon.
- rapha: nothing affected Evil's aim, not damage, not knockback; he kept hitting hard, which broke everyone's
  "formula" of how much damage a fight would cost. His weakness: rail-heavy players.
- k1llsen: unshakeable mental, kept his flow at low HP; slightly weak at aggressive punishment.
- ZeRo4: knows when to push and when he has the edge; very confident.
- Evil on rapha (Wikipedia, paraphrased): rapha sets up sequences of armor pickups, takes advantageous
  positions and plays a standard, safe game: effective against weaker players, predictable against strong ones.
- Evil on cypher (QuakeCon 2015 interview, via translator): cypher always plays sneaky and accurate, not
  aggressive; Evil was used to it. He lost his first map by an early mistake that cypher punished for two frags,
  then "figured out what to do" and took the initiative.

### 1.11 strenx (Spain; QL)

LG specialist of the Quake Live era (k1llsen pairs his LG with k1llsen's own rail as the weapons nobody wanted to
face). Source: his streamed "Quake Live Duel Lessons #1 - Positioning" (full transcript).

- If you are behind, be "Pacman": pressure the opponent, take all the items, force him to camp and take
  nothing; when you are 200/200, rush.
- On Campgrounds (t7) the first 20 to 30 seconds decide most of the game: whoever takes control early usually
  wins. Rail is the key weapon there: with rail and the opponent without it, you have position everywhere,
  especially over the mega, and do damage without taking any.
- From the start he reasons by elimination: check one place, check another, nothing for two or three seconds,
  so at second 23 "he will be here" for sure.
- With a bad spawn while the opponent holds the red: do not rush stupidly. Survive, waste his time, make him
  spend time hunting you, so that the next red and the mega come up together and you can contest.
- After a kill, ask what he will do on respawn: weapon first or item first? A player without a weapon is
  harmless, so deny weapons first. "It's all about weapons", taking them and preventing him taking them. Use
  sound: hearing him take a yellow tells you where he goes and that he has no weapon.
- When out of control, force the two big items to come up at the same time (keep pressure so he cannot take
  the mega on time); then take the one he cannot cover.
- Against a mega spammer, pressure with rail or LG, moving forward and back.
- Keep mid-range against a player without LG.
- Force spawns by your position (fake a move to make him spawn where you want).
- On Toxicity, time one yellow and the mega: with a yellow timed you control the map, because the opponent
  never goes to the red without control (he would die), so he takes the yellow. At the mega, "don't rush it
  first, you just check and you spam". Never fight LG against LG from the worse position; switch to rockets.
- Tricks: fake a drop to make a waiting opponent commit, then take the other route.
- Mental: in 2011 he played without raging, "camp camp camp", and played better because he stayed focused.

### 1.12 clawz (QC, QuakeCon 2017 champion)

Source: Inven interview. Aim-reliant (Vo0 called his LG "the best in the world"; Raisy lists him among players
who depend on aim). He played as aggressively as possible on LAN to test how far it would go, because he
expected opponents to play safer and they struggled with his aim. Quake Live experience told him when to
attack and when to defend. Under 30 hours of QC duel practice. rapha (Team Liquid): clawz is "too
overaggressive"; rapha prepares for his pushes and punishes overextension.

### 1.13 dkt (Daniel Kapadia, UK) as a theorist, and Av3k

dkt is a former pro and the interviewer of rapha's legacy video. His "Quake Duel Theory" lecture is the most
systematic statement of duel theory I found from a player, so it is included.

- Two parts to winning: execution and decision-making. Execution is inherently inconsistent (you will not hit the
  same percentage every game), so it is a poor basis for planning; decisions can be quantified (weapons, position,
  stacks, surprise all have values).
- Three states: in control (your stack so dominant he cannot contest any item you choose), out of control
  (you must react and play around him, always keeping escape options), contested control (most interesting
  decisions). The state can shift whenever damage is dealt or an item is taken; recognise the shift quickly.
- Stacks are never known exactly: estimating the opponent's health and armor is a skill.
- Efficient engagements: judge every fight by net damage given its weapons, position, surprise, the stacks and
  the items spawning. A stacked player may take an inefficient fight on purpose, "paying" stack to buy an
  item or a kill; the out-stacked player cannot.
- Trading rails is fine for the stacked player and terrible for the out-of-control one: a trade sets the weak
  player back a minute from any major item. After a surprise shot, leave; do not stay for the second shot.
- Weapons by job: rocket for choke points, close range, long-range spam, baiting items (not long-range duels);
  LG for airborne targets and close to mid range; rail for mid/long attrition and for the out-of-control player
  who must not get close.
- Items spawning change fights: steer a fight so you can rocket-jump to the red the moment it spawns, or use the
  fight to waste time and reposition onto the mega.
- Death costs most on big maps with lots of armor (ZTN, Lost World): out of control, never die first; then learn
  to deal damage while not dying; that is the hard part. Small maps (Aerowalk) trade faster.
- Positioning controls the opponent's movement (dm13: the control player leaves items up to keep the opponent
  trapped); high-ground spots with a drop-away (dm6 bridge over the red) let a weak stack take engagements safely.
  The opponent's knowledge of your weapon (you have LG in the open middle, he does not) changes where he can go.
- A beginner-and-pro method: time only the mega (35 s) and run the armors in a fixed order, so their spawns follow
  your pickup order. The mega matters more because armor takes two thirds of damage and health one third
  (he prefers 200 health / 100 armor to the reverse).

Av3k (Polish, QL/QC top player, Endpoint host) adds: focusing on rotations (rotate the armors, time one yellow
and rotate the red with it) rather than only the red and mega turned his ZTN around; small changes make big
differences; "game sense" is repeated situations producing a gut feeling of what the opponent is doing.

### 1.14 Others (thin coverage)

- **Fatal1ty**: found nothing in his own words on item control or timing; only career and mindset pieces (Vo0
  says he copied Vo0's always-moving style in Painkiller and was a strong spawn fragger).
- **Spart1e**: on ESReality's timing thread he says he usually times two items, sometimes three or four
  "depending on situation", and that a third item is easy when focused but makes other decisions harder.
- **DaHanG** (USA): rapha describes his style as timing-based and a little aggressive, unlike Europeans who

- **gellehsak, serious, w1nny, Xron, ProZaC**: nothing substantial from their own words was found. Serious

## 2. Themes across players

### 2.1 Where they agree

1. **Decisions beat aim at the top.** ZeRo4 ("purely his decision making"), cypher and Av3k (when and where, not
   how), Raisy ("not the most important aspect"), rapha (most top-eight players out-aimed him), dkt (execution is
   unreliable). Aim is necessary (toxjq: practice is useless if you cannot hit enough to defend yourself), but
   above a threshold the differences are small and the decision differences large.
2. **The stack decides the fight before it starts.** Cooller narrates stacks; cypher and Av3k count stacks and
   say you know who wins before the fight; dkt builds his theory on it; rapha builds his QC style on the
   armor/health split. Exception: an aimer like Evil, or a lucky knockback (Vo0), can break the arithmetic;
   that is why it is a probability.
3. **Timing is both for owning items and for locating the opponent.** Everyone times; the number of items differs
   (one to five). Timing is most valuable when out of control (cypher, Av3k, rapha on dm6).
4. **Sound is a primary sense.** Footsteps, jumps, pickups, rocket jumps, weapon pickups: rapha, Cooller,
   cypher, k1llsen ("every single sound cue"), strenx, Raisy ("hear my opponent out"). Silence is information
   too (k1llsen, rapha confirming Cooller was not jumping).
5. **Out of control, survive first.** cypher ("trying not to lose"), dkt (do not die first), Raisy (camp and wait
   a cycle), strenx (survive and waste his time), rapha (keep timing, take no damage). Chip damage only when it is
   free; leave after a surprise shot.
6. **Deny weapons as well as items.** Cooller denied rapha the rocket; strenx: "it's all about weapons"; rapha
   took the rail rather than the red so Cooller would not get it free.
7. **Read habits, set traps.** toxjq, cypher, rapha, k1llsen (and the converse: hide your habits, k1llsen stopped
   streaming practice; toxjq avoided bootcamps).
8. **Think ahead; plan the next 10 to 30 seconds.** cypher (moves that pay off 20-30 s later; you cannot live in
   the moment), rapha (five steps ahead; what happens if I die), strenx (what will he do on respawn).
9. **Mental: do not gamble to win back a loss.** rapha, k1llsen (lost nerve, attacked without backup), Raisy
   (let go), czm (talk aloud to stay on the game), strenx (no rage).
10. **Practice variety, review, honesty.** rapha and cypher both warn against practising mainly with one partner
    or only with stronger players.

### 2.2 Where they differ

- **Control vs aggression.** Cooller's constriction and AGENT's item-heavy control vs strenx's and clawz's
  aggression. rapha's own path shows the answer depends on the game's rules: defensive control in QL (armor
  stacks to 200, big items decide), aggressive armor cycling in QC (soft caps). k1llsen won QC with the opposite
  of rapha's QC style (slow, central, traps). Raisy moved from aggressive to passive as the game's patches changed.
  Conclusion: no single style wins; the style follows from the rules of armor, weapons and spawns, and from the
  opponent.
- **How much to time.** rapha 3-5 items; cypher long timed only the mega exactly; dkt and some top players
  time only the mega and run armors by pattern; Spart1e two, sometimes more. All agree timing must not crowd out
  reading the opponent (rapha: you then "play the map").
- **Playing the map vs playing the player.** rapha's framing; he claims his edge was doing both.
- **When ahead.** rapha and Raisy run the clock and avoid risky frags; AGENT (per Av3k) is the one who always tries
  to frag even with control; strenx says rush at 200/200. rapha's rule: attack only when a second kill is near
  certain, and weigh where the dead opponent will respawn.
- **Aim style.** Rail-standing aimers (k1llsen, Stermy) vs moving all-round aimers (cypher, toxjq per toxjq).

## 3. How the game changed: Q3 to QL to QC

- **Quake 3 (CPMA and VQ3).** Armor and health stack to 200/200; few items decide; spawn killing was more common
  (rapha); high-ping practice trained prediction aim in players like Cooller, cypher and ZeRo4 (rapha's theory).
- **Quake Live.** Same item logic as Q3 duel with tuned weapons (LG damage changed, hitboxes changed; rapha struggled
  for a period when the game became more aim-favouring). The "big four" era of control, exact timing (red 25 s,
  mega 35 s after it wears off) and reading. Control on maps with three major items (dm6) is hardest to break
  (rapha: only three majors, so a controller can hold all of them).
- **Quake Champions.** Champions with abilities and different stacks; round duel (three lives) made play risk-averse
  with long overtimes (k1llsen); time-limit duel brought back catch-up (rapha, k1llsen). Armor soft caps mean you
  cannot hoard; rapha won by cycling armors with small fights. Patches swung the game between speed and position
  (Raisy). rapha calls QC weapons overpowered compared with QL.
- **Quake Live again (2023-2025, 250 FPS league).** cypher back and winning against AGENT, whose style is item-heavy
  control with frequent attempts to frag.

## 4. Principles distilled

Each principle lists who stated it; links are in section 6 (short keys in brackets).

1. **Die less than the opponent within the time limit; everything else serves that.** rapha [R-legacy].
2. **The resource stack decides most fights before they start; fight when your stack and position give you the
   edge, not when your aim might.** cypher and Av3k [C-secret], Cooller [Coo-ESL], dkt [dkt-theory], rapha
   [R-legacy].
3. **Know the stack split: armor absorbs about two thirds of damage, health one third; health-stacked players win
   all-ins, armor-stacked players should take many small fights.** rapha [R-legacy], dkt [dkt-theory].
4. **Time items, at least the mega and the item you are fighting for; time the opponent's items when out of
   control; timing tells you where he must be.** rapha [R-ESL][R-reflect], cypher and Av3k [C-secret][C-agent],
   dkt, Spart1e [ESR-timing].
5. **Do not let timing replace reading: track the items and the opponent's likely position together.** rapha
   [R-reflect].
6. **Listen. Sounds (steps, jumps, pickups, weapon pickups, rocket jumps) and their absence locate the opponent and
   reveal his stack and weapons.** rapha [R-ESL], Cooller [Coo-ESL], k1llsen [K-reflect], strenx [S-lesson],
   cypher [C-secret], Raisy [Raisy-FoV].
7. **Estimate the opponent's stack from what you saw him take and what you dealt.** dkt [dkt-theory], Cooller
   [Coo-ESL], cypher [C-secret], czm (second-hand) [ESR-timing].
8. **Out of control, play not to lose: survive, keep escape routes, chip only free damage, leave after a surprise
   shot, wait for a cycle where his items sync up or he gets impatient.** cypher [C-secret], ZeRo4 via rapha
   [R-reflect], dkt, Raisy, strenx.
9. **Force his big items to spawn together, then take the one he cannot cover.** strenx [S-lesson], rapha [R-ESL].
10. **Delay his pickups (fight near the item, cost him seconds) to push his timings where you want them.** rapha
    [R-ESL], Cooller [Coo-ESL], critpoints analysis.
11. **Deny weapons, not only armor; a player without the right weapon cannot fight at the right range.** Cooller
    (denying rapha the rocket) [R-ESL], strenx [S-lesson], dkt [dkt-theory].
12. **Choose weapon by range and situation: rail for range and attrition, LG for mid and close and airborne
    targets, rockets for chokes, splash, spam and denial; never fight the opponent's best weapon at its best
    range from a bad angle.** dkt [dkt-theory], strenx [S-lesson], cypher [C-agent].
13. **Position is the multiplier: high ground with a drop-away, holding the angle he must pass, playing backwards
    so he walks into your fire.** rapha [R-ESL][SteelSeries], cypher [C-secret], dkt, toxjq [Tox-2].
14. **Value your life by what happens after your death: before a fight ask where he respawns, where you respawn,
    and whether you can retake control.** rapha [R-reflect], strenx (what does he do on respawn) [S-lesson].
15. **When ahead, do not force a risky extra frag; attack only near-certain fights and run the clock; sometimes
    leave him alive to keep him on a bad respawn or away from an item.** rapha [R-legacy][R-reflect][R-ESL],
    Raisy [Raisy-FoV].
16. **Do not gamble to win back a lost chance; tilt makes the next spawn worse.** rapha [R-legacy][R-reflect],
    k1llsen [K-reflect].
17. **Read habits and set traps; hide yours; avoid hard reads with no leeway.** toxjq [Tox-2], cypher [C-secret],
    rapha (via ZeRo4) [R-reflect], k1llsen [K-reflect].
18. **Adapt to the opponent: speed up against a poor timer, slow down against an impatient player; feel him out in
    the first minutes; readjust when something fails.** rapha [R-legacy], k1llsen [K-reflect].
19. **Think ahead of the present moment; see the map from above.** cypher [C-secret], Cooller (via Av3k), rapha
    [SteelSeries].
20. **Neither pure running nor pure holding: rotate the items and think where the opponent is and his next move.**
    ZeRo4 [Z-2013].
21. **Mechanics must be subconscious so attention is free for the game.** czm [czm-IEM].
22. **Review and varied practice; no single practice partner; play lost games out.** rapha [R-legacy], cypher
    [C-secret].

## 5. Implications for Bobby

Framing, as the project rules require: nothing below proposes making Bobby copy a pro's choices, rates, or places.
The players' statements are used here as (a) descriptions of what information and reasoning the game rewards,
and (b) measurable facts about the game and about human senses. What Bobby does with them must come from his own
pay for winning games. Where a pro statistic appears (rapha timing three to five items), it is a readout of what
a human can track, not a target.

### 5.1 What he should be able to perceive

- **Sound as a first-class sense.** Every player above relies on it, and k1llsen's best event was "every single
  sound cue". Bobby should hear what a person hears: footsteps (and the absence of them when the opponent walks),
  jump grunts, pickups by item type (mega, red, yellow, weapon, ammo, health), rocket jumps, weapon fire, pain
  sounds, teleporter use, with direction and rough distance, human-level noise and delay. Silence must be
  representable (no sound from an area for N seconds). The project already gives enemy projectiles only as seen
  or heard; the same principle should cover the opponent's item pickups.
- **His own item clock.** Timing is unanimously essential, but a human times from memory, not from a server
  table. Inputs should give what he could know: when he saw or heard each major item taken (and by whom), not
  the true respawn time if he did not perceive the pickup. Whether he then remembers it is the memory's job.
- **Pickup-delay facts.** Since players delay pickups to shift the opponent's timings, the item's actual
  pickup second matters, not only its spawn second; the input must carry "taken at" not "spawned at".

### 5.2 What he should be able to estimate and remember (the brain's job)

- **The opponent's stack.** Every top player estimates it (dkt calls it a skill; Cooller narrates it). The brain's
  predictions should include the opponent's health and armor as a belief (with uncertainty), updated from what he
  saw him take, what he heard him take, damage dealt, time since respawn (he respawns at 125/0), and small items.
- **The opponent's weapons and ammo**, from pickup sounds, seen weapon, and spawn. strenx's whole respawn logic is
  "does he have a weapon yet".
- **The opponent's item timers**: when the items he controls will come back, so Bobby can see when two of them sync
  (strenx, rapha).
- **The opponent's position as a belief**, narrowed by elimination (strenx's "checked there and there, so he is
  here"), sound, last sighting, and his likely route.
- **The opponent's habits within a game** (does he double back, does he always take the jump pad, does he rush
  weapon or item first on respawn). This is toxjq's and cypher's core skill. Within-game adaptation fits the
  brain's memory; across-game memory of a person is the owner's "later" item.
- **Where each player will respawn** (or the distribution), since rapha prices every fight by the respawn.

### 5.3 What he should decide (the head's job)

The pros describe a small set of strategic choices made every few seconds, which matches the head's 0.2 s order:

- **State: in control / contested / out of control**, recognised quickly from the two stacks and the item
  timers (dkt). Each state has different good orders.
- **Orders that must exist and be cheap to choose** (several already in the project's list): hold an angle over
  an item that is about to spawn; take or deny a weapon; delay his pickup; force or wait for an item sync; hide and
  survive a cycle; chip from range and leave; cut off a route; spam a choke or a predicted point; break off a
  fight; run the clock; and, importantly, *leave him alive* (do not chase a frag) when the respawn or an item would
  cost more than the frag gives.
- **Fight-or-not as a stack-and-position judgement**, not an aim judgement: cypher and Av3k's arithmetic suggests
  the head should see both stack estimates, the expected damage rate of each weapon at the current range and angle
  (a measurable fact of the game, not a pro's choice), and the exit routes.
- **The price of death** should enter the decision through the win-chance value, which already sees the score and
  time. rapha's rule (what does it cost if I die here, given where we both respawn) is exactly what a good value of
  the state after death would encode; so the value needs inputs that make respawn consequences visible (the
  respawn belief, item timers, stacks).

### 5.4 What he should be paid for

- **Winning games stays the goal.** The players agree that frags and damage are means. rapha leaving the opponent
  alive, Cooller taking the mega to die rather than let rapha stack, cypher at 17 HP skipping every item: all are
  choices a damage or frag pay would punish and a win pay would allow. This supports the project's direction of
  a head paid only by the win chance.
- **The win-chance judge should know the stack and control state.** If the judge sees only score and time, it will
  not value control, and the head will not learn that a 200/200 stack with all timings is worth more than a frag.
  The pros' unanimous view that the stack decides fights suggests the judge's inputs should include both stacks
  (the true ones in training are fine for a judge that is not a player input), item timers, and weapons.
- **Beware pay that rewards "doing damage"**: the Serious critique (taking fights that maximise damage and accuracy
  but not kills, fighting stacked opponents on aim) is the behaviour a damage pay would produce. cypher's "trying
  not to lose" defence deals damage too, but only as a by-product of surviving.
- **Beware pay that rewards pickups for their own sake**: rapha's point that in QC hoarding fails, and the
  timing-as-locating point, both say an item is worth what it does to the next fights. A pickup pay is a shaping
  pay; the win-chance judge should carry the real value.

### 5.5 What the pros say about learning, applied to self-play

- **Practice partner syndrome.** rapha and dkt both lost events after practising mostly against one player: they
  learned risks that only worked against him. For self-play this is the case for the league, the control in every
  check, and opponents of different styles (a constrictor, a runner, a trapper, a camper), so Bobby does not learn
  exploits of his own habits. Measuring against several fixed opponents, map by map, is the pro's "play many styles".
- **Adaptation inside a game.** rapha's first-five-minutes feel-out and k1llsen's readjust-until-it-works are what a
  recurrent policy with a memory can learn, if the opponent's habits vary across training games.
- **Playing out lost games.** rapha's practice of finishing lost games to learn comebacks maps onto the project's
  mid-game starts from real moments, including starts where Bobby is out of control.
- **Execution is unreliable; decisions are not** (dkt). The fair-aim limits mean Bobby's execution will be
  human-like and noisy; the pros say this is where decisions win games, which supports investing in the head and
  the brain rather than in aim.

### 5.6 Measurable facts from this research (readouts only)

These can be shown beside Bobby's numbers as labelled readouts, never as targets:
- Number of items a top human tracks: one (the mega) to five (rapha's claim on Aerowalk; ESR forum, second-hand).
- How long humans can hide and deal no damage out of control: rapha says staying hidden one to two minutes is
  very hard.
- Time scale of a control cycle: two to four minutes of near-full control at most in QC (rapha); five-plus
  minutes to break out of control in QL finals (rapha's dm6, cypher's Aerowalk).
- The share of games with full control at pro level: about one in twenty (rapha's estimate).
